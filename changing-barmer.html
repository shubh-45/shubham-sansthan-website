A district in motion

A changing Barmer

Breaking the old chains, and walking fast towards a more modern and more reasoning way of living.

The Thar Desert region of Rajasthan, and Barmer district in particular, has historically been known for its rich cultural inheritance — and, alongside it, for deep conservatism: the ghoonghat, child marriage, the death feast, and the absence of schooling.

That is changing. In the present era, Barmer is breaking those chains and moving quickly towards modernity and towards reason. The principal aspects of that positive change are set out below.

  1. Education spreads — and girls are the point

    • Literacy improves. Through the efforts of the district administration and of voluntary organisations, the number of schools and colleges in rural areas has grown.
    • Daughters take flight. Where girls were once kept within the four walls of the house, the daughters of Barmer now take higher degrees and enter the administrative services — IAS, IPS, RAS — the police and the army.
    • A blow to superstition. Education has made the thinking of the new generation scientific and reasoned, and reliance on exorcism and tantric ritual has fallen accordingly.

  2. The campaign against child marriage and the ghoonghat

    • Child marriage declines. Awareness committees are active at village level to protect the lado — the daughters — and to stop child marriage. Between the administration and a network of alert young people, even secret child marriages are now being prevented.
    • A society free of the veil. In many villages, women public representatives and forward-looking sarpanches have begun to give up the ghoonghat altogether, and women's participation in public life has risen with it.

  3. Curbing the death feast and ruinous spending

    • Boycotting mausar. The caste panchayats of Barmer's various communities — Jat, Rajput, Meghwal, Bishnoi and others — have placed strict prohibitions on the death feast, a custom that was expensive enough to drown families in debt.
    • Simplicity at weddings. Rules are being made to stop wasteful spending, dowry display and the rest of it at weddings, and to put that money into the education of children instead.

  4. Economic self-reliance and the digital shift

    • Refinery and industry. The Pachpadra refinery and the solar and wind energy projects have opened new routes to employment for local young people. As families become economically stronger, they step out of the old rigidities.
    • Handicraft and women's empowerment. Thousands of rural women connected to Barmer's celebrated embroidery and Ajrakh print are self-reliant today, and are making the decisions in their own families. Ruma Devi, an artisan of international standing, is the face of exactly this change.

  5. Alertness against superstition — official and social

    • Health services reach further. With primary health centres opening in the villages and ambulance services arriving, people now go to a doctor when they are ill, rather than to a tantrik.
    • A connected younger generation. Social media and digital connectivity have joined young people in Barmer's most remote dhanis to the rest of the world, and they are shedding superstition and adopting modern thinking as a result.
Where we come into it

Almost every line above describes work that somebody had to actually do — village by village, year after year, usually without being paid for it. Awareness programmes, health information, girls' schooling, rights education, and the slow business of getting a village to agree to give something up. That is the work Subham Sansthan has been doing here since 2000.
